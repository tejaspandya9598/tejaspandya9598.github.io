01 — Signal research

Averaging your alpha signals loses money

Six cross-sectional signals on 494 S&P 500 names, 2010–2026, scored by Spearman rank information coefficient with Newey–West corrected t-statistics. Only Amihud illiquidity cleared significance standing alone. Low volatility and the lottery signals came out with the wrong sign.

So the obvious move — z-score everything and average — produces a Sharpe of −0.58 and a 74% drawdown. Learning the combination under purged walk-forward CV with a one-month embargo, charging 10 bps a side, reaches 0.77.

Limitation: the universe is current index members extended backward, so it is survivor-only. That biases the signal rankings — the illiquid names that survived are the ones that grew. I would want point-in-time constituents before defending any single t-statistic.

02 — Portfolio construction

A conclusion that survives a 20× move in its own assumption

Position sizing posed as a convex program carrying transaction costs inside the objective rather than subtracted afterwards: turnover as a rotated second-order cone, square-root market impact as a 3-D power cone, MOSEK-first with an open-source conic fallback; on this machine CLARABEL solved all 494 per cost level, none failing to reach optimality.

The impact coefficient is assumed, not fitted from tick data. So the useful test was sweeping it. Drag the chart: the multi-period line barely moves across the whole 0–100 bps range while the frictionless book collapses.

What changed when I fixed it: this used to say the frictionless book wins below ~12.5 bps. That crossover was an artefact — the backtest was allocating each month on macro data published the month after. With the publication lag applied the multi-period book wins at every cost level in developed markets, and emerging still crosses, between 0 and 5 bps. A finding about where two lines cross is a finding about the alpha feeding them.

03 — Derivatives pricing

Three methods, one interface, and the gap between them documented

A dependency-free C++20 engine valuing the same contract three independent ways: closed-form Black–Scholes with analytic Greeks, Monte Carlo with exact terminal sampling, and a Crank–Nicolson finite-difference solver on a 400×400 grid — Thomas algorithm for Europeans, projected SOR on the early-exercise complementarity problem for Americans.

They agree to roughly 0.6% on an at-the-money call, pinned as regression tests. On barriers they don't, and that disagreement is the interesting part.

Where they diverge: discretely-monitored Monte Carlo prices an up-and-out call at 3.5162 against the continuously-monitored finite-difference value of 3.3326. That is the discrete-versus-continuous monitoring gap. It shrinks as step count grows, and I report both rather than the flattering one.

04 — Market microstructure

The O(1) claim, with its fine print

A deterministic limit order book and matching engine in C++20 enforcing strict price–time priority. Resting orders sit in intrusive doubly-linked lists per price level, so cancelling is a pointer splice rather than a search. A pre-warmed memory pool means the matching loop never allocates — which is what keeps the tail flat, not just the mean.

Prices are fixed-point unsigned integers with no floating point on the hot path, because float comparison in a price-priority book is a correctness defect rather than a slowdown.

The qualifier: "O(1) for everything" isn't true. Inserting at a new price level is O(log L) — the ordered map has to track the touch. Four of five hot-path operations are O(1); the fifth fires whenever the book opens a level it didn't have.

05 — Rates

P&L attribution that has to add up

A SOFR discount curve bootstrapped from par swap quotes by forward substitution with log-linear discount-factor interpolation, marking a ten-swap book of $255M notional from 2 to 30 years. Every input par swap reprices to under 1e-9.

Daily P&L then decomposes into carry, roll-down and curve movement by projecting key-rate sensitivities onto orthogonal level, slope and curvature shapes — with convexity and off-shape misfit booked as an explicit residual, so the parts reconcile to a full revaluation rather than approximately.

Scope: a single SOFR discount curve. No multi-curve basis, no XVA, no cross-currency. The 1e-9 figure is internal self-consistency, not a comparison against any external pricer.
